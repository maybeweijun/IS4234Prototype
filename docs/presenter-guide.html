Ready-to-read speaker scripts

1. Autosteer safeguard assurance

0.5 min / Chen Junyao

Good afternoon. We are Group 21, and our project examines Tesla's Autosteer safeguards and the governance of its corrective software updates.

Our proposed product, Safeguard, connects safety evidence to accountable release decisions. We will explain the case, analyse the GRC weaknesses, and demonstrate how the prototype supports independent review and corrective action.

Sources: Supplied project report, sections 1 and 4; Supplied project specification, pages 5-7

2. The assurance question

1 min / Chen Junyao

Our central question is: how should evidence of foreseeable driver misuse shape release and corrective-action decisions?

The scope is Tesla's Autosteer safeguards in the United States. Autosteer operates as part of a Level 2 driver-assistance system, so continuous driver supervision remains essential. That makes the adequacy of the safeguards supporting supervision a critical assurance question.

Our core period runs from August 2021 to April 2024. We identify the May 2024 information request as follow-up evidence, rather than treating it as information available when the December remedy was released. We also exclude wider Full Self-Driving controversies and unrelated recalls. This gives us a defined product, a defined safety claim and a defined decision process to examine.

Sources: Supplied project report, sections 1-2

3. Investigation and remedy chronology

1.5 min / Chen Junyao

There are four developments to highlight in the chronology.

First, the preliminary investigation began in August 2021. It was expanded into an engineering analysis in June 2022, with further attention to driver engagement and human factors.

In December 2023, Tesla filed Recall 23V838, covering approximately 2.03 million US vehicles, and delivered additional safeguards through an over-the-air software remedy.

In April 2024, NHTSA closed that engineering analysis while opening a recall query to assess the remedy's adequacy. That combination matters. Completing the original investigation and distributing a remedy did not settle the separate question of whether the remedy adequately addressed the underlying concern.

We should also be precise about the evidence: opening a query warranted further investigation. It did not establish that every part of the remedy had failed.

Sources: https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2023/RCLRPT-23V838-8276.PDF; https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/inv/2022/INCLA-EA22002-14498.pdf; https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/inv/2024/INOA-RQ24009-12046.pdf

4. Existing safeguards and the evidence gap

1.25 min / Chen Junyao

Tesla already had engagement monitoring, driver warnings and restrictions. The remedy strengthened or added controls, so the issue was their adequacy and operation.

For example, a warning can trigger correctly without producing an appropriate driver response. An update can also be installed while an optional safeguard is disabled or later reversed. This means installation, activation, driver behaviour and safety outcomes answer different questions.

The assurance process must connect those questions to the actual software version and vehicle configuration being considered. It must also recognise trade-offs, including false alerts, privacy concerns and restrictions on legitimate use.

That is why our product focuses on the evidence supporting a decision, rather than treating deployment completion as proof of effectiveness. Christopher will now explain the root cause and the GRC implications.

Sources: https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2023/RCLRPT-23V838-8276.PDF; https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/inv/2024/INIM-RQ24009-12199.pdf

5. Root cause and the GRC challenge

1.5 min / Chong Jia Cheng Christopher

The report's strongest diagnosis is a mismatch between the supervision the system requires and the safeguards supporting that supervision. Foreseeable driver inattention therefore needs to be treated as a design and assurance consideration.

The associated GRC challenge is ensuring that the combined evidence supports an accountable judgment. Engineering may confirm that software behaves as specified. Validation may complete its test plan. Compliance may check reporting records. Those activities do not automatically establish that the same safety claim has been adequately demonstrated.

A release authority needs to understand the configuration gaps, contrary findings and remaining uncertainty before accepting residual risk. Significant disagreements must remain visible and have a route for escalation.

We are not claiming that public records reveal precisely how Tesla's internal approval process broke down. The fragmentation risk is our analytical implication from the case. Our recommendation is to make ownership and the basis of each decision explicit.

Sources: https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/inv/2022/INCLA-EA22002-14498.pdf; Supplied project report, sections 3.1-3.6

6. Frameworks clarify different responsibilities

1.25 min / Chong Jia Cheng Christopher

We use three perspectives to organise that analysis.

COSO helps us ask whether authority, risk assessment, evidence requirements and monitoring operate as a coherent control process. A dashboard alone would not address a weak control environment or a lack of authority to challenge delivery decisions.

The Three Lines Model clarifies responsibilities. Engineering retains operational ownership. Safety and compliance specialists provide challenge. Internal audit assesses whether the process works, rather than routinely approving releases it may later audit.

ISO 21448's published scope adds a complementary perspective on functional insufficiency and reasonably foreseeable misuse.

These frameworks have limits. They do not supply our numerical prototype thresholds, prove Tesla adopted a particular framework, or establish certification. We use them to structure the questions and responsibilities while retaining qualified engineering and safety judgment.

Sources: Supplied project report, section 3.3; https://www.theiia.org/globalassets/site/about-us/advocacy/three-lines-model-updated.pdf; https://www.iso.org/standard/77490.html

7. Regulatory duties and internal assurance

1.25 min / Chong Jia Cheng Christopher

The regulatory obligations and our proposed internal controls need to be distinguished.

The report discusses defect notification and remedy obligations under US motor vehicle safety law, alongside recall reporting and campaign records. It also explains why later safety-related changes may require further assessment of the original remedy and its regulatory treatment.

Our evidence gates, independent approvals and corrective-action workflow are proposed internal controls supporting responsible decisions. They are not a claim that legislation prescribes this exact application or these exact thresholds.

This distinction also explains our implementation scope. Legal safety-change classification and recall-campaign reconciliation remain supporting organisational controls. The prototype concentrates on the evidence and decisions surrounding safeguard assurance. It does not make a legal determination, submit a filing to NHTSA or certify regulatory compliance. Those responsibilities still require competent people and authoritative records.

Sources: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2022-title49/html/USCODE-2022-title49-subtitleVI-partA-chap301.htm; Supplied project report, section 3.4

8. Four controls make the decision accountable

1.25 min / Chong Jia Cheng Christopher

The proposed response is expressed through four controls from the report.

C2 requires relevant, current evidence for the required scenarios and configurations. Missing or inadequate evidence must have a consequence for the decision.

C3 requires an independent reviewer to record a reasoned release judgment. This makes acceptance of residual uncertainty attributable.

C4 requires reassessment when the software build or supporting evidence materially changes. An old approval should not silently continue to apply to a different decision basis.

C5 requires adverse signals to produce owned follow-up, a due date, escalation and independent closure. Better data alone should not make an earlier concern disappear.

Together, these controls connect the case analysis to testable operations. The next part shows how Safeguard implements them. Wei Jun will introduce the users, architecture and review rules before our demonstration.

Sources: Supplied project report, section 4.2, Table 5; contracts/SafeguardAssurance.sol

9. Role-specific workspaces

1 min / Tan Wei Jun

Safeguard presents a focused workspace for each responsibility.

The engineer submits validation evidence, investigates assigned concerns and records material build changes. The field analyst supplies post-update comparisons and can report a credible concern.

The safety reviewer inspects evidence, makes release judgments and independently closes actions. The safety lead monitors ownership, deadlines and escalation. The auditor has a read-only view for reconstructing decisions and exporting the record.

Shared candidate information keeps these users aligned, while irrelevant controls are hidden to reduce confusion. Regulators can receive reviewed evidence packages without becoming routine internal release approvers. In this prototype, the role picker selects test identities. Actual transaction permissions are still checked by the smart contract.

Sources: web/index.html; web/app.js; contracts/SafeguardAssurance.sol

10. A shared record of evidence and decisions

1.25 min / Tan Wei Jun

This diagram separates the interface, transaction service, contract and evidence storage.

The browser sends a request to the signer service. The service uses ethers to submit a signed transaction to the Solidity registry on a local Hardhat EVM. The registry checks the rules and records the evidence revision, reviewing address and resulting events. Source documents and canonical evidence records stay off-chain.

The potential blockchain benefit is a common record that independently governed participants can verify. It reduces exclusive reliance on one operator's editable database. However, a conventional application with signed, append-only logs could provide similar workflow controls when one trusted operator is sufficient.

Our local network demonstrates the contract behaviour. Production trust would require independent network governance, authenticated signing and protected evidence storage. A real release service would also have to enforce the contract's current decision before distributing software.

Sources: scripts/lib/prototype.ts; scripts/serve.ts; contracts/SafeguardAssurance.sol; https://docs.soliditylang.org/en/latest/security-considerations.html

11. Demonstration rules preserve uncertainty

1.25 min / Tan Wei Jun

The rules deliberately preserve uncertainty instead of converting missing information into a pass.

For laboratory evidence, every required scenario and configuration needs accepted results for the current build. The illustrative policy requires at least thirty trials and ninety-five percent successful responses.

For field evidence, both cohorts need sufficient exposure, complete and comparable data, active safeguards and no serious-event flag. The selected event rate must not increase.

A release judgment additionally requires an independent reviewer, a rationale, no open actions and a validity period of no more than thirty days.

These are demonstration rules taken from the report's proposed workflow. They are not Tesla criteria, legal thresholds or validated safety requirements. Passing means eligible for reviewer assessment. It does not establish that rare harm is acceptably unlikely. We will now demonstrate how those distinctions affect the decision process.

Sources: Supplied project report, section 4.5; contracts/SafeguardAssurance.sol

15. Feasibility depends on operating controls

1.25 min / Justin Chan Wee Hsien

Feasibility has several dimensions.

Technically, the local prototype shows that the selected workflow can execute. Operational use would need trustworthy connectors, durable evidence storage, authenticated signing and integration with the release process.

Operationally, reviewers need appropriate expertise and sufficient time. Teams also need reliable escalation and an equivalent controlled process during an outage.

Organisationally, a sponsor must establish decision rights and protect independent challenge when it conflicts with delivery pressure. A well-designed interface cannot compensate for routinely ignoring adverse findings.

Economically, the pilot must measure integration effort, reviewer time, maintenance and hosting costs. We have not assigned a financial return because Tesla's internal costs and baseline error rates are unavailable. The recommendation is therefore a limited pilot with measurable review benefits, rather than immediate enterprise-wide deployment. Urgent hazard containment should continue through existing safety processes while that pilot is developed.

Sources: Supplied project report, sections 4.1 and 4.7; docs/solution.html

16. An accountable rollout

1.25 min / Justin Chan Wee Hsien

The rollout begins with accountable safety action, rather than waiting for a new platform.

Immediately, safety and engineering leaders should assess urgent hazards and preserve the basis of interim-control decisions.

During the first thirty days, the safety sponsor should agree approval authority, reviewer independence and minimum evidence requirements.

The next stage is a pilot covering one safeguard family. The validation lead should examine scenario and configuration coverage and resolve the gaps. Engineering systems and compliance teams can then reconcile versioned records and test protected access.

Wider adoption should depend on evidence that the process improves review quality and corrective-action timeliness without an unmanageable burden. Independent assurance should examine whether the process is actually followed. These periods are planning assumptions from our report. They are not a claim about a completed Tesla implementation or a guaranteed delivery schedule.

Sources: Supplied project report, section 4.1, Table 4

17. Trust and implementation limits

1 min / Justin Chan Wee Hsien

The implementation limits are important to the recommendation.

A fingerprint can show whether evidence changed relative to a trusted recorded hash. It cannot establish that the original measurement was truthful or that the study was appropriate.

A wallet address also does not establish human independence. Production use needs qualified identities, protected keys and organisational separation. Sensitive incident records and cabin data should remain in controlled off-chain storage.

The local Hardhat network is resettable, and the current application does not control vehicles or deliver OTA updates. A protected release service must enforce current authority for the actual software artifact and scope. Our result is therefore a functioning assurance workflow with clear operational requirements, rather than a claim of regulatory approval or proven vehicle safety.

Sources: docs/solution.html; Supplied project specification, pages 6-7

20. Questions to prepare for

Appendix

[Q&A PROMPTS: use the relevant response rather than reading every answer.]

Why blockchain? Its potential advantage is independent verification of a shared record. A conventional application with signed logs may be sufficient when one trusted operator is acceptable.

Could a reviewer approve false evidence? Yes. The contract cannot establish whether a study is truthful or appropriate. Trusted sources, qualified review and organisational controls remain necessary.

Does approval prove safety? No. Our rules establish workflow eligibility for a human judgment. They are illustrative and do not demonstrate acceptable rare-event risk.

What happens when the evidence changes? Current authority is invalidated and another decision is required. Closing a concern and authorising release remain separate judgments.

How would this affect actual OTA delivery? A protected deployment service would have to match the artifact and configuration scope, then enforce the current contract decision before distributing software.

Sources: contracts/SafeguardAssurance.sol; Supplied project specification, pages 5-6

21. Blockchain and a conventional web system

Appendix

[OPTIONAL RESPONSE TO THE BLOCKCHAIN COMPARISON QUESTION]

A conventional web application can also enforce approval rules, hash evidence and maintain signed, append-only logs. We should not present those capabilities as unique to blockchain.

The difference is the trust arrangement. With independently governed validators, participants can verify a common ordering of evidence and decisions without relying exclusively on one organisation's database administrator. If one operator effectively controls the network, that advantage is much weaker.

Both approaches still depend on reliable source evidence and qualified human judgment. The current local prototype demonstrates signed transactions and rule execution. It does not yet demonstrate a durable, independently governed production network.
